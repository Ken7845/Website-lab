# Brand Voice

## Core Feeling

Quiet, observant, personal, and image-led.

The voice should feel like someone looking closely at ordinary life and noticing the emotional texture inside small visual moments. It should not sound like marketing copy, a portfolio pitch, or a professional bio. It should feel calm, reflective, and specific.

---

## Perspective

Use a first-person perspective when the writing is about process, memory, or observation.

The speaker can be present, but not performative. The writing should reveal attention through concrete details rather than explaining too much about the self.

---

## Tone

- Calm and restrained
- Observational rather than promotional
- Personal without being overly sentimental
- Direct, but with room for atmosphere
- Slightly poetic when describing light, color, time, or material behavior

---

## Language Patterns

Prefer:

- Specific visual details
- Short declarative sentences mixed with slower reflective lines
- Sensory language around light, color, weather, material, and movement
- Process language that shows trial, uncertainty, and discovery

Avoid:

- Generic portfolio claims
- Overconfident self-branding
- Empty adjectives such as "best", "professional", "innovative", or "passionate"
- Explaining the work too neatly
- Forcing a call-to-action tone

---

## Example Voice

"The first frame. I'm after a specific green -- the deep, saturated green of this tree against a summer sky. The instant film decides otherwise."

This voice works because it is concrete, visual, and quietly personal. It lets the image-making process carry the emotion.

---

## Site Writing Direction

Ken's Gallery should read like a living visual notebook: a place for photography, daily observations, experiments, and musings. The writing should support the images instead of overpowering them.

---

## Typography and Layout Voice

The site’s typography should feel like a quiet archive rather than a portfolio pitch.

Use a restrained editorial system:

- Large site and section titles should follow the Works title language: Boldonse as the primary display face, heavy but quiet, with calm spacing and no decorative exaggeration.
- Do not substitute a “similar-looking” display sans by feeling. If a page needs to match Works, read the actual Works title attributes first and reuse that system across breakpoints.
- Small labels, numbers, dates, and category text should feel archival: compact, precise, and low-volume.
- Body text should stay calm and readable, with enough line height for reflection.
- Serif text can appear as a quiet secondary voice for notes, descriptions, or reflective fragments, but should not become decorative.
- Avoid oversized marketing hierarchy where every section tries to announce itself.

The preferred rhythm is:

```text
large quiet title
small archival label / number
measured paragraph
image or work entry
```

Spacing should let the reader pause. The page should feel observed, not performed.

For Home and Works, the type system should connect:

- Home introduces the atmosphere.
- Works organizes the archive.
- Both should share the same typographic calm: soft white text on dark surfaces, tight display tracking, subtle metadata, and stable row/grid structure.

For image-led sections, preserve the integrity of the work image. The frame should scale with the image’s own proportion when completeness matters; do not crop a work into a fixed holder just to make the grid mathematically even.

Avoid:

- Loud display fonts that make the page feel like a poster.
- Too many font personalities on one page.
- Section titles that feel unrelated to the archive rows.
- Phone layouts where numbers, titles, or metadata collide because desktop typography was simply squeezed down.
